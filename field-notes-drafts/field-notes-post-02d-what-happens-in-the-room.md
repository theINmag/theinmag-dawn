# what happens in the room (the launch, the check-ins, the Learning Pit).

By Ryan - Co-founder, theINmag. Former numeracy specialist and advocate for rich, creative learning.

5 minute read

---

> **Quick answer.** The lesson itself has three moves that matter most: a 60-90 second launch that lands the prompt; multiple check-ins where one kid's strategy becomes a doorway every other kid can walk through; and the nerve to let kids sit in James Nottingham's Learning Pit without rescuing them. Get those three right and the room teaches itself.

---

This is part 4 of the open numeracy task series. Parts [1](/inside/open-numeracy-task-great-place-to-start), [2](/inside/planning-an-open-numeracy-task), and [3](/inside/trajectory-of-learning) covered the why, the planning, and the sequencing.

This post is about what happens between the bell going and the bell going again. The lesson itself.

If you've planned well, you've already done the harder half. But three moves in the room separate a deliberate open-task lesson from one that floats. Get these right and the lesson runs itself.

## The launch.

Before you get to the messy middle, you have to land the start.

The launch is where you read the prompt out, build a tiny bit of intrigue, and signal that this is a thinking lesson. You can write the prompt on the board with a single underline. You can ask one kid to read it aloud. You can mention it's a question lots of mathematicians have argued about.

The launch is short. 60 to 90 seconds. Its only job is to get every kid into the same starting blocks, leaning forward.

Then you let them go.

## The check-ins (yes, plural).

This is where most of the real teaching happens.

A check-in is when you stop the class, gather them around one kid's solution, and ask the kid to explain their thinking. Not the answer - the thinking.

"Billy, you wrote 4 x 25. How did you get there?"

Then to the rest of the class: "Could you do that with a different number? What if I asked you to make 80 using the same idea?"

The check-in turns one kid's strategy into a doorway every other kid can walk through. You'll do this multiple times in a single lesson, depending on what you're seeing - once early when an interesting strategy surfaces, once mid-lesson to share a misconception worth wrestling with, once near the end to celebrate something unexpected.

This is also where one of the most powerful moments in any classroom can happen.

There's often a kid in your room who's been quietly told - by themselves, by the system, sometimes by other adults - that they're "not a maths kid." During an open task, this is the kid who suddenly thinks of something nobody else thought of.

*That's* the strategy you bring to the check-in.

The whole class goes "wow" and so does the kid. Their entire self-image as a learner can flip in 90 seconds. I've watched it happen. I would teach this way for that moment alone.

## The teacher as thinker, not rescuer.

Doug Clarke at ACU has spent years pointing out that primary teachers, with the best intentions, often rescue kids out of productive struggle. A kid puts up their hand and says "I don't know what to do" and the teacher's instinct is "well, you could start by..."

Resist this.

Try "tell me one thing you've tried." Try "what if it was a smaller number - could you do it then?"

The aim is to keep the thinking on the kid's side of the table, not relocate it to yours. Australian researcher Martin Renton has written some of the best practical guidance on questioning techniques in primary classrooms - worth a deep dive once you've taught a few of these.

The hardest version of this rule: even when you can SEE the answer, even when the kid is one nudge away, even when the lesson is running short - hold. Wait. Ask one more question. The kid who arrives at the strategy themselves owns it forever. The kid who's handed it forgets it by Friday.

## The nerve to let kids sit in the Learning Pit.

James Nottingham's Learning Pit is the framework that names what every teacher feels in this part of the lesson.

The kids climb in. They get confused. They struggle. They feel a bit lost. And as the adult in the room, your every protective instinct screams *get them out*.

Don't. The pit is the lesson.

The struggle is where the connections form. A kid who answers 100 worksheet questions correctly hasn't learned much. A kid who gets 100 wrong hasn't either. The learning happens in the engaged, uncomfortable middle - where they're testing, explaining, hearing other kids explain, abandoning ideas, and trying again.

Sitting with that discomfort, as the adult, is part of the practice. It gets easier. It never gets easy.

The honest version: many teachers were trained to be the calm, helpful, problem-solving adult in the room. Open tasks ask a different version of you. The version that's confident enough in the kids' thinking to let them be uncomfortable for ten minutes. That's a craft, and it takes practice.

## A note on assessment.

Look at the strategies, not the answers.

A simple rubric with three columns - "uses one strategy" / "uses multiple strategies" / "extends or generalises a strategy" - tells you more about a kid's number sense than 20 column-addition questions do. Photograph their working. Sample three or four kids each lesson. Over a unit you've got rich formative evidence that tells the story of how each kid actually thinks.

This is the assessment system that closed-task teaching cannot give you. Worth the switch for that reason alone.

## What happens by week three.

The first time you run an open task, half the room is uncomfortable. They keep asking "is this right?" The lesson takes longer than you planned. You doubt yourself.

The second time is smoother. The kids start asking "what if I tried..." instead of "is this right?"

By the third time - usually around week three of consistent practice - the room has shifted. Kids enter the lesson assuming there are multiple paths. They check each other's strategies. They argue. They self-correct.

That's the moment the room becomes a maths classroom in the truest sense. The kids are doing the thinking. You're doing the noticing. The learning is theirs.

That's why this is worth all of the planning, all of the trajectory work, all of the discomfort.

## Frequently asked questions

**My class isn't used to open-ended tasks. Where do I start?**

Start short. Ten minutes the first time, not forty. Use a prompt with a very low floor (like "make 20" for year 2, or "make 100" for year 4) so every kid can enter. Don't expect the third-week magic on day one - it builds through repetition. The discomfort is real on both sides, kids and teacher, and the second session is always smoother than the first.

**How do I assess this if there's no single right answer?**

Look at the strategies, not the answers. A three-column rubric (uses one strategy / uses multiple / extends a strategy) tells you more about number sense than 20 column-addition questions ever could. Photograph kid working. Sample three or four kids each lesson. By the end of the unit you've got rich evidence that tells the story of how each kid thinks.

**What about the kids who hate the openness?**

Some kids who tie their identity to being "good at maths" find open tasks disorienting. Lower the floor for them privately. "If you're stuck, just write down the easiest one you can think of, then make it slightly harder." Almost always works. The discomfort fades by week three.
