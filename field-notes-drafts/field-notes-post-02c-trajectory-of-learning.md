# the trajectory of learning (or why one good open task isn't enough).

By Ryan - Co-founder, theINmag. Former numeracy specialist and advocate for rich, creative learning.

4 minute read

---

> **Quick answer.** A great open task on its own teaches less than three okay tasks built into a sequence. The trajectory of learning - what comes before, what comes after - is what most open-task teaching skips. The good news: a well-planned task tells you exactly what to teach next. The misconception that surfaces today writes tomorrow's lesson.

---

This is part 3 of the open numeracy task series. [Part 1](/inside/open-numeracy-task-great-place-to-start) made the case for the genre. [Part 2](/inside/planning-an-open-numeracy-task) covered the planning craft. This post is about the move that separates a one-off engaged lesson from a sequence of unforgettable ones.

The move is sequencing. Building a trajectory.

It's also, I think, one of the main reasons inquiry-style teaching has had a rocky run in Australian classrooms over the past two decades. The philosophy is right. But teachers were rarely shown how to build the trajectory of learning that sits underneath it. Without that trajectory, inquiry-flavoured lessons become "let kids explore" with no through-line, and learning becomes patchy.

This post is about how to do it properly.

## What's a trajectory of learning?

It's a sequence of lessons designed so that what surfaces in lesson 1 becomes the question of lesson 2.

Not "I'm teaching place value this week so all my lessons are place value."

More like: "Lesson 1 asked kids to make 100 in as many ways as possible. The misconception that surfaced was about measurement - kids confusing 100 cm with 100 m. Lesson 2 takes that misconception outside, gets them measuring, and resolves it."

The lesson 2 plan doesn't exist until lesson 1 has happened. That's the point. Most planning treats lessons as independent. A trajectory treats them as a conversation with the kids' actual thinking.

## A worked example.

Real lesson sequence, lightly anonymised.

**Lesson 1.** Year 4 class. The prompt: "How many ways can you make 100?"

During the check-ins, two interesting things surfaced. The first was a kid who confidently wrote "100 minutes = 1 hour and 40 minutes" then crossed it out and wrote "wait, 1 hour is 100 minutes? No, 1 hour is 60 minutes. Why?"

The second was a different kid who wrote "100 metres" and "100 centimetres" as two separate ways to make 100, then got into a quiet argument with their partner about whether they were the same thing.

Two doors. Either could become the next lesson.

**Lesson 2 (the day after).** Outside, on the school oval, with measuring tapes and stopwatches.

The prompt: "How fast can you run 100 metres?"

The kids measured 100 metres themselves. They felt what 100m is in their bodies. They ran it. They timed each other. The "100m vs 100cm" misconception evaporated within ten minutes - the centimetre kid stretched out the tape, looked at it, looked at the oval, and said "oh."

Then the timing data brought the second misconception in for free. Kids ran 100m in around 18-22 seconds. Someone went "wait, my time is 0.3 minutes? Are minutes like 100 seconds? Why are they 60?"

Two misconceptions. One lesson. Built directly off what surfaced the day before.

That's a trajectory.

## Why this matters more than the lessons themselves.

A lesson that surfaces a misconception but doesn't resolve it has done half a job. A misconception that doesn't get resolved within a week tends to harden into a hard-to-shift belief. That's especially true in numeracy, where so much of the subject is non-intuitive in the first place.

But a sequence that surfaces a misconception today and resolves it tomorrow does something almost magical. It teaches the kids that their thinking matters. The teacher noticed it. The teacher built the next lesson around it. The kids are quietly being told: *we're doing real maths here, your half-formed ideas are part of the work.*

This is also why open tasks get more powerful in the third week of using them, not the first. The trajectory compounds. Every lesson is built off the last. The kids are visibly more confident by week three because they're seeing themselves shape the curriculum.

## The honest catch.

This kind of teaching is harder to schedule. Term planners in most schools want lesson 2 finalised before lesson 1 has been run. That doesn't quite work for trajectory teaching.

The fix isn't to abandon term planning - it's to plan in pencil. Identify the prompts and the broad sequence in advance. Leave the specifics of lesson 2 (and 3, and 4) to be shaped by what surfaces. This requires trust from your school leadership. Worth asking for.

## Frequently asked questions

**My school requires me to submit term plans with all lessons finalised in advance. Can I still do this?**

Yes, but it requires a conversation with your leadership. The version that works: submit the prompts and the broad sequence, but flag that the specific lesson plans for lessons 2 onwards will be finalised based on what surfaces in lesson 1. Most leaders are fine with this once you explain the pedagogy. Some aren't. Pick your battles.

**What if no interesting misconception surfaces?**

Then your next lesson is whatever you'd planned. No harm done. But in my experience, an open task always surfaces something. If nothing's surfacing, the prompt might be too closed - try a more open one next time.

**Does this work for non-numeracy subjects?**

Yes. Trajectory thinking works for literacy (writing surfaces what kids need next in mini-lessons), science (an inquiry surfaces the conceptual gap to teach next), and most learning that's about thinking rather than memorising. The principle is the same: today's lesson tells you tomorrow's.
