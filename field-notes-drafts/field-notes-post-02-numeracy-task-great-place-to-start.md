**Topic:** Numeracy

# an open numeracy task is a great place to start.

By Ryan - Co-founder, theINmag. Former numeracy specialist and advocate for rich, creative learning.

4 minute read

---

> **Quick answer.** Open-ended numeracy tasks aren't a Plan B you reach for when the worksheet bores you. The evidence has been overwhelming for thirty years that this is how kids actually learn maths. If you're new to the genre, "how many ways can you make 100?" is one of the gentlest entries. But running it well is harder than running a worksheet, and that's the catch most posts skip.

---

I want to head off something at the start. Open-ended numeracy tasks aren't a thing you reach for when you're sick of your worksheets. They're not a Plan B. They're not a flash of inspiration on a Wednesday afternoon when the kids are restless.

The evidence has been overwhelming for thirty years. Rich, open tasks teach kids more than closed practice does. They build flexible thinking. They reach the kids who get left behind by procedural drill. They ask kids to do the actual work of mathematics, which is *thinking*, not *recalling*.

Running them well is a craft. Like any craft, the public part (the lesson itself) is the smallest fraction of the work.

This post is the start of a four-part series for teachers and homeschoolers who are curious about open numeracy tasks but unsure where to start. We'll work through the planning, the trajectory of learning, and what actually happens in the room. None of it is a recipe. All of it is grounded in real Australian primary classrooms.

## What is an open numeracy task?

It's a prompt with multiple correct answers, multiple solution paths, and no single procedure that gets you to "the right one."

A closed task: "What is 47 + 53?"
An open task: "How many ways can you make 100?"

Same general territory. Completely different mathematical experience.

A closed task tests whether the kid can do a thing. An open task asks the kid to think about the territory the thing lives in. Which is what real mathematicians do.

Peter Sullivan, who's been writing about challenging tasks in Australian primary classrooms for decades, describes them as tasks where every kid in the room can enter at their own level, AND there's no upper limit on where the most curious kid can take it. That double access is the gold.

## Why "make 100" is a useful starting point.

Two reasons.

**One.** The prompt has thousands of solution paths. A kid lands on 50 + 50 and feels like a maths kid for the first time that week. A kid lands on 0.5 + 0.5 and finds fractions. A kid lands on "1000 cents in a $10 note divided by 10" and finds money. Every kid in the room can enter, and the ceiling is wherever the most curious kid lifts it to.

**Two.** The prompt doesn't just unlock number sense. It quietly unlocks place value, fractions, decimals, money, time, the metric system, and operation choice - all in a single sentence. None of which is in the wording. All of which surfaces when kids start exploring.

A worksheet exposes a kid to one idea, ten times. An open task exposes them to ten ideas, sometimes only once each - but those connected exposures across different contexts are exactly how durable knowledge gets built.

## What's the catch?

Just one, but it's a big one. Doing this well is harder than running a worksheet, not easier.

The planning is more demanding. You're not picking ten questions of escalating difficulty - you're anticipating every possible direction a class of 28 thinkers might take a single prompt. The in-the-moment teaching is more demanding too. So is the assessment. So is the differentiation.

Almost every guide on open tasks I've ever read skips this. They make open-ended tasks sound like the easy option for an engaged classroom. They're not. They're the harder option that produces deeper learning. Worth doing for that reason alone, but worth doing properly.

## Frequently asked questions

**I've heard "open task" and "rich task" used interchangeably. Are they the same thing?**

Mostly yes. "Open task" describes the prompt structure (multiple paths, multiple answers). "Rich task" describes the mathematical depth (genuine thinking, multiple concepts). A good open task is also a rich task. A genuinely rich task is almost always an open one.

**Where does an open task fit in my curriculum?**

Whichever curriculum you're working from - Australian Curriculum, the Victorian Curriculum, the NSW syllabus, your homeschool registration framework - an open task usually maps to multiple descriptors at once. "Make 100" can hit place value, additive strategies, multiplicative strategies, fractions, decimals, money, time, and measurement, depending on where the kids take it. Pick the strongest descriptor for your reporting and let the rest live in your unit-level notes.

**Is this just inquiry learning rebranded?**

Related but not identical. Inquiry learning is a broader pedagogy that often spans subjects and weeks. An open task is a single prompt, runnable in a single lesson, that shares some of inquiry's design DNA. You can run open tasks without fully committing to inquiry learning as a model.
