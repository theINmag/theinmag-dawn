## Concept summary

Inquiry-based learning has had a rocky two decades in Australian primary classrooms. Beloved by some teachers, distrusted by many, blamed by media, defended by academics, often poorly implemented. PISA scores get cited. The Reading Wars get cited. Teachers get caught in the crossfire.

Ryan's argument: the philosophy was right. The rollout missed the trajectory of learning. Without that trajectory, inquiry-flavoured lessons became "let kids explore" with no through-line, and predictably the learning was patchy. Critics correctly identified the patchiness. Defenders correctly identified the philosophy. Both sides were right about half of it.

This post is meant to be an honest, practitioner-led contribution to a conversation that's been mostly polemical. NOT a defence of inquiry. NOT an attack on it. A diagnosis from someone who's stood in classrooms, planned the lessons, and watched what happens when the trajectory work is and isn't there.

Key beats:
- Open with a real moment from Ryan's classroom. A lesson that DID work. Why it worked. The planning that sat behind it.
- The honest framing: I'm not here to tell you inquiry is brilliant or terrible. I'm here to tell you that what most schools rolled out under the inquiry banner was missing the bit that makes it work.
- The diagnosis: the philosophy of inquiry (kids drive their own learning, knowledge is constructed, real questions matter) is well-supported by research. The IMPLEMENTATION required a piece of teacher craft most teachers were never properly trained in: building the trajectory of learning so that today's lesson tells you tomorrow's. Without that, it really did become "let them explore" and the kids really did learn less.
- A nod to the critics: the OECD scores, the cognitive load research, John Hattie's effect-size data. These critiques have force. Pretending they don't is bad faith.
- A nod to the defenders: the kids in well-run inquiry classrooms genuinely do learn more, retain longer, and report higher engagement. Pretending they don't is also bad faith.
- The reconciliation: it's not philosophy vs implementation. It's that the implementation requires a level of teacher craft most school systems didn't invest in. The trajectory of learning is the missing skill. Once teachers have it, inquiry teaching works exactly as advertised. Without it, the best-intentioned inquiry classroom is patchy.
- Pasi Sahlberg's quote on Finland - where the trajectory is built into teacher training and school culture, not bolted on as a curriculum directive.
- The way forward: a humble one. Take inquiry seriously. Train the trajectory work. Don't pretend the criticism is just culture-war noise. Don't pretend the philosophy is fashion.
- Close with: theINmag is a practitioner project, not a polemic. Every issue is built around open prompts that work because the trajectory is thought-through. That's our small contribution to the conversation.

## FAQ candidates

1. Are you saying my school's inquiry approach is wrong? (Answer: no. I'm saying it's harder to do well than the rollout often suggested. Schools doing the trajectory work are getting strong outcomes. Schools that aren't, aren't. The diagnosis isn't ideological.)
2. What do parents do when they think their kid's school is over-relying on inquiry? (Answer: ask the school to walk you through the trajectory of learning for a specific unit. If they can show it, the kid is in good hands. If they can't, that's the conversation to have. Politely.)
3. Is theINmag pro-inquiry or anti-inquiry? (Answer: neither. theINmag is pro-craft. The pedagogy that produces real learning, whatever you call it.)

## Risk notes

- Steel-man both sides. The critics' research is real. The defenders' classroom evidence is real.
- Don't name specific schools, ministers, or commentators. Critique frameworks, not people.
- Personal anecdote where possible - "in my classroom" not "all classrooms."
- Close with the contribution, not the critique. theINmag is doing X. That's the point.
