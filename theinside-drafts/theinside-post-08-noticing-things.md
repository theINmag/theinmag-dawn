## Concept summary

Anchored in theINmag's Snaps (photography) submissions section, but the post is actually about a much bigger underrated skill: noticing. Kids who notice things - a weird shadow, a bug nobody else saw, the way the wind moves the curtain - make better writers, better photographers, better artists, better humans.

The post sits at the literacy / photography crossover and gives parents and teachers a small, practical move to encourage noticing. Doesn't need to be photography. Can be writing. Can be drawing. The skill is the same.

Key beats:
- Open with two real Snaps submissions - the most striking one and the most ordinary-but-arresting one. Names withheld or first names only.
- The reframe: "noticing" sounds passive, like nothing's happening. But noticing is the precondition for every creative act. You can't write about something you didn't see. You can't draw what you weren't looking at. The whole edifice of creative work sits on top of this one tiny skill.
- Why most kids LOSE their noticing skill around age 6-8. Same dip pattern as the creativity post - they start filtering out the "boring" world to fit in with peers who do the same. The kid who keeps pointing at things adults walk past is teased mildly, and the pointing slows down.
- The fix: model it. When you're walking the dog, point at one weird thing every walk. The shape of a leaf. A cloud that looks like a sandwich. A crack in the path that resembles a face. Don't make a project out of it. Just point.
- Lucy Calkins quote on observation as the backbone of children's writing - her "writers notice" workshops are arguably the most influential primary literacy framework of the last 30 years.
- Optional: Maggie Dent on slow attention as a wellbeing skill.
- Close with: every Snaps section in theINmag is a 12-page reminder that Aussie kids are STILL noticing. Their parents and teachers haven't quite let them lose the skill yet. Send IN your kid's snap of the weirdest, smallest, most-walked-past thing they've spotted this week.

## FAQ candidates

1. My kid only photographs blurry close-ups of grass. Is this useful? (Answer: yes! Close-up grass IS noticing. The skill builds quietly. You don't need their submissions to be art-gallery-ready. They just need to be paying attention.)
2. Do I need to buy them a camera? (Answer: no. A phone camera works fine. A drawing of what they noticed works fine. A description in a notebook works fine. The medium isn't the point.)
3. What if they never want to notice anything? (Answer: try noticing things AT them rather than asking them to notice. "I just saw the weirdest thing on that fence" lands better than "what do you notice?" 9 times out of 10.)
