# planning an open numeracy task (the bit nobody told us about).

By Ryan - Co-founder, theINmag. Former numeracy specialist and advocate for rich, creative learning.

4 minute read

---

> **Quick answer.** Planning an open task is more demanding than planning a worksheet, not less. You're anticipating every direction 28 kids might take a single prompt, then pre-loading next moves for each. Four anticipations matter most: entry points, rich misconceptions, differentiation (which lives in your back pocket, never on the board), and what's next for each kid.

---

This is part 2 of the open numeracy task series. [Part 1](/inside/open-numeracy-task-great-place-to-start) made the case that running these well is harder than running a worksheet. This post is about why.

The planning is the harder half. Get it right and the lesson runs itself. Get it wrong and the room flounders. The four anticipations below are what separates a deliberate open-task lesson from one that hopes for the best.

## Anticipating entry points.

Where will the most reluctant kid in the room start? Probably 50 + 50, or 99 + 1.

Where will the most confident? Probably 4 x 25, or 1000 / 10, or "let me try fractions and decimals."

You need both ends covered before the bell goes. A kid who can't enter the task disengages within 90 seconds. A kid who finishes the obvious answer in two minutes does the same. Both ends are equally bad.

The fix is anticipation. Sit with your team and brainstorm: what are the 5-10 most likely starting points kids will choose? What does the strugglingest kid in the room do first? What does your most confident kid do first? Now you've got a map.

## Anticipating the rich misconceptions.

These are the kid responses that look like errors but are actually doors into bigger ideas.

Some kid will write "100 minutes" and call it an hour. Wrong - because an hour is 60 - and now you've got the conversation about why time is one of the only number systems we still use that isn't base 10.

A kid will write "100 cm = 1 metre" and now you're inside the metric system, asking why we measure the way we do.

Another will write "$1 = 100" - which is true in cents, and a brilliant door into place value and money.

None of this is on the worksheet. All of it is real numeracy. You plan for these because you want them to surface during the check-ins. The "wrong" answers are often where the deepest thinking happens.

## Anticipating differentiation. Privately, never publicly.

This is the bit teachers most often get wrong, and it matters more than the others combined.

The worst thing you can do is start an open task by inviting kids to choose their own difficulty level. The moment you do that, the room reorganises around anxiety. Kids who already feel shaky about numeracy now have to publicly declare it. Kids who tie their self-worth to being "the smart one" now have to perform.

Everyone starts at the same place, with the same prompt, grappling with the same thing. *That's* where the beauty comes from.

The differentiation lives in your back pocket and gets deployed in one-on-one conversations, mid-lesson, when you're already kneeling next to a kid. "Tell me one thing you've tried." "What if it was a smaller number - could you make 20 first?"

These are private moves. The kid never knows you've intervened. They keep their dignity and their access to the task.

Pre-write the enabling prompts (for kids who need a smaller floor) and the extending prompts (for kids who hit the ceiling fast) before the lesson. Have them in your head, ready to deploy. But never on the board.

## Anticipating what's next for each kid.

This one is the hardest. It's also where most open-task teaching falls down.

For Maya who confidently writes 25 x 4, what does she try next? For Jack who's struggling with 50 + 50, what's the smallest possible step that makes him feel like a maths kid?

You don't always get this right in real time - but you've thought about it, so when the moment comes, you have somewhere to go. The kids who progress fastest in open-task classrooms are the kids whose teachers have spent five minutes thinking about each of them before the lesson.

Five minutes per kid is twenty minutes for a class of four kids you're focusing on this week. Worth it.

## The thing that makes all four work.

Sit with your planning team and *do the task yourselves*. Not in a "let's quickly check this works" way. Genuinely. With your kid hats on.

Solve it ten different ways. Fight with it. Notice where you wanted to cheat. Notice where you got stuck. Notice the moment you went "oh, hang on…" and a new door opened.

Living the task with your own kid-hat on is how you build a real trajectory of learning - because now you actually know where the deep ends are. The next post in this series goes into that trajectory work, and it's the bit that turns one good lesson into a sequence of unforgettable ones.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long does this kind of planning take?**

Honestly, more than you'd expect at first. A 60-minute open lesson might take 45-60 minutes of team planning the first time you run that prompt. The good news: most prompts are reusable, year after year, with the planning compounding each time. By the third year of running "make 100" with your year 4s, you can plan it in 10 minutes because you know every door.

**My team doesn't have time to plan together. Can I do this solo?**

Yes, but the team version is meaningfully better. Two teachers thinking about a prompt with their kid hats on will find doors a single teacher misses. If team planning isn't possible, plan with one other teacher, even informally. A 20-minute coffee conversation about a prompt unlocks more than two hours of solo planning.

**What if a kid takes the task somewhere I didn't anticipate?**

Celebrate it loudly during the check-in. Tell the kid (and the class) that they found a door you hadn't seen. This is the best possible outcome of an open task - it teaches the kids that thinking is the goal, not finishing, and it teaches you something new about the prompt for next year.
